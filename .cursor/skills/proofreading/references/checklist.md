# Proofreading checklist

Read this before writing the report. Use it to decide *why* something is an error and how to phrase the suggested fix. Do not dump this list into the user-facing report — translate matches into numbered items with location, issue, and fix.

Match the document's existing dialect and house style when one is clear. If the piece mixes conventions without a clear majority, flag under Consistency rather than "correcting" to your preferred style.

## Spelling and word usage

- Typos and misspellings (including proper names established earlier in the piece)
- Homophones: their/there/they're, your/you're, its/it's, to/too/two, affect/effect, than/then, whose/who's, complement/compliment, principal/principle, counsel/council
- Wrong word that is still a real word (form/from, quiet/quite, loose/lose, peek/peak/pique)
- Repeated or missing words ("the the", dropped articles that break the sentence)
- Accidental word doubling across a line break or paste

## Grammar

- Subject–verb agreement (including intervening phrases: "The list of items *are*" → *is*)
- Verb tense drift within a passage that should stay in one tense (unless the time shift is intentional)
- Pronoun agreement and unclear antecedents (number and gender; "this/that/it" with no clear referent)
- Article usage (a/an/the) where omission or the wrong article is an error, not a stylistic choice
- Faulty parallelism in lists and paired constructions ("to run, jump, and swimming")
- Dangling or misplaced modifiers that attach the wrong agent to the action
- Comparative/superlative forms used incorrectly
- Incomplete comparisons ("better than *anyone*" vs "*anyone else*" when needed)

## Punctuation

- Comma splices (two independents joined only by a comma)
- Run-ons and fused sentences
- Accidental sentence fragments (keep intentional fragments in sermons, dialogue, and punchy prose — flag only when the fragment looks like a mistake)
- Missing or stray commas in compound sentences, introductory clauses, and nonrestrictive asides
- Serial (Oxford) comma: do not impose a preference; flag only inconsistency within the piece
- Apostrophes: possessives vs plurals; it's/its; plural possessives
- Quotation marks: punctuation placement consistent with the piece's dialect; quotes opened and closed; quote-within-quote nesting
- Dashes and hyphens: hyphen for compound modifiers where needed; en/em dash usage consistent with the piece (do not rewrite dash style to preference)
- Colons and semicolons used where a comma or period was needed, or vice versa, when the current mark breaks the sentence
- Ellipses and terminal punctuation: triple-dot style consistent; no double periods

## Capitalization and numbers

- Mid-sentence common nouns capitalized without cause
- Proper nouns and titles left lowercase inconsistently
- Headings: match the document's existing heading case pattern; flag drift, don't invent a new pattern
- Numbers: consistency of words vs numerals for similar quantities in similar contexts; avoid starting a sentence with a raw numeral if the rest of the piece spells those out
- Percentages, dates, times, and scripture/citation references: internal consistency of format

## Consistency (piece-wide)

Scan the whole document, not just the current paragraph:

- British vs American spelling (*organise/organize*, *centre/center*) — pick the dominant form; flag outliers
- Terminology: the same concept named two ways without reason (e.g. "congregation" / "church family" may be intentional; "login" / "log-in" / "log in" as noun/verb often is not)
- Hyphenation of the same compound across occurrences
- Serial-comma policy, quote-mark style (straight vs curly if visible), and list punctuation (period vs no period on list items)
- Heading and list style: parallel grammatical shape across sibling headings/items
- Emphasis patterns: bold/italic used for the same kind of thing the same way
- Character or place name spelling locked after first appearance

## Paragraph construction (evaluate, then suggest)

These are suggestions, not automatic fixes. Quote the split/merge point.

- **Unity:** one governing idea per paragraph; split at the sentence where a second idea begins
- **Length:** walls of text that bury the turn; or a string of one-line paragraphs that chop one thought — judged against *this* piece's rhythm, not a universal word count
- **Topic clarity:** opening sentence should orient; if the real topic arrives two or three sentences in, note it
- **Transitions:** abrupt landings with no link to the prior paragraph; missing bridge when the logic jump is large
- **Merge candidates:** adjacent micro-paragraphs that clearly continue one thought
- **Reorder:** only when two adjacent sentences or paragraphs are clearly swapped in a way that breaks sequence (rare; usually a Note if the fix would rewrite)

## Out of scope (Notes only)

Do not number these as fix candidates unless the user later asks to address them:

- Voice, tone, or style preference ("sounds stronger if…")
- Argument structure, missing evidence, theological or factual claims
- Content cuts, expansions, or reordering of ideas beyond a clear accidental swap
- "I'd phrase this differently" rewrites when the sentence is already grammatical and clear
